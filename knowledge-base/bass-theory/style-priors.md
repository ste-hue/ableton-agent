# Style priors

What these styles *value* — the concerns their conventions came from. They are
priors, not instructions: useful for orientation, useless as templates. A brief
that names a style is telling you what the person cares about, not asking you
to reproduce a canonical pattern.

Two failure modes to avoid. The obvious one is pastiche: emitting the textbook
pattern for the name. The subtler one is ignoring the priors entirely and
producing something generic — the values below are real, they came from
players solving real problems, and a groove that honours them will feel right
even when it looks nothing like the archetype.

Tempo ranges are observations about where these styles usually sit, not
constraints. Interesting music happens at the edges and outside them.

---

## Boom bap and its descendants

*Usually 85-95.* Values a **pocket**: the sensation that the beat is slightly
behind where you expect while still keeping time. Values **interplay over
density** — the parts answer each other rather than all playing at once, and
the gaps are where the interplay is audible. Values **asymmetry**: hits that
don't repeat identically bar to bar, timing that isn't quantized, drums that
sound like objects rather than samples. Values **space for a voice**, because
the beat was always a bed for rapping — the midrange stays clear and the
pattern leaves room at the ends of phrases.

The characteristic tension is between a *hard* backbeat and *soft* everything
else. Dynamic range is the instrument.

## Trap and modern hip hop

*Usually written 130-150, felt at half.* Values **the tension between fast and
slow**: a backbeat that moves at half the speed of the hats. Values the
**hat as a lead voice** — it is where the rhythmic invention lives, and rolls
and subdivision changes are gestures, not decoration. Values **sub weight**:
the bass is a pitched, sustained instrument, and often the only harmony.
Values **emptiness** — most of the bar is silence, which is what makes the
sub feel enormous.

## House

*Usually 118-128.* Values **continuity**: the pulse never stops, which is
what lets a dancer stop paying attention to it. Values **the off-beat** — the
lift between the pulses is where the genre's energy lives. Values **gradual
change**: elements enter and leave over many bars, and a listener should not
be able to point at the moment something changed. Values **groove borrowed
from disco and funk**, which is why swing and shuffle often sit under a
metronomic pulse.

## Techno

*Usually 130-145.* Values **relentlessness and hypnosis**: repetition long
enough to stop being repetition. Values **timbre over pattern** — the same
figure with a filter opening across sixteen bars is the composition. Values
**machine precision** rather than human feel; this is the one family where
straight quantization is the aesthetic and not a failure. Values
**subtraction**: the drama comes from removing things and putting them back.

## Drum & bass and jungle

*Usually 168-176.* Values the **break**: a chopped, re-sequenced acoustic
drum performance, so the source is human playing and the treatment is
surgical. Values **half-time feel over double-time drums** — the backbeat is
slow, the ghost notes and rolls are fast, and both are true at once. Values
**the sub as the melodic centre**, carrying pitch and movement while the
drums carry rhythm. Values **edit as composition**: reversals, stutters,
chops that would be impossible to play.

## Reggaeton and dembow-family

*Usually 88-100.* Values the **dembow**: a syncopated backbeat pattern felt in
3+3+2 against a steady kick, so the groove is a rhythmic *cycle* rather than a
bar of hits. Values **the cycle over the bar** — the pattern's identity is the
whole loop, not the individual placements. Values **percussive dryness and
midrange bite**, which is why the snare/rim is often the loudest element.

## Afrobeats and afropop

*Usually 100-118.* Values **layered percussion with distinct roles**: several
parts, each simple, whose interlock produces a rhythm none of them plays
alone. Values **syncopated kick placement** rather than a downbeat anchor —
the pulse is implied by the ensemble, not stated by one drum. Values
**shakers and light metal** as the timekeeper instead of a hat pattern. Values
**a bass that is melodic and conversational**, answering the vocal rather than
doubling the kick. (Fela-era Afrobeat is a different, older, jazz-inflected
music with horn sections and long forms; if someone means that, they will
usually say so.)

## Funk, soul and live drumming generally

*Usually 90-115.* Values **the one**: everything is measured from the first
beat of the bar, and the drama is in how far parts can stray before returning
to it. Values **ghost notes as continuous motion** — the snare hand never
really stops, it just gets quiet. Values **dynamics inside a single bar**.
Values **the sound of a person getting slightly tired**, which is why
mechanical consistency reads as wrong here more than anywhere else.

---

## Using these

When a style is named, ask what the person is after: the *values* (usually) or
the *artifact* (sometimes — "make it sound exactly like a 90s boom bap
record" is a real request). If it's the values, compose freshly with those
concerns in mind and let the result be its own thing. If it's the artifact,
say that you're going for the archetype, do it well, and offer a variation
that departs from it.

Combining priors is where the interesting work is: boom bap's pocket applied
to a techno pulse, afrobeats' interlocking roles at drum & bass tempo. Nothing
in this file forbids it.
